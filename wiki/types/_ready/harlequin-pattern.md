# Harlequin Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/harlequin-pattern/profile/harlequin-pattern-image.svg)
A repeating pattern of contrasting diamond shapes — derived from the costume of the Harlequin character in 16th-century Italian Commedia dell'arte, and commercially active in fashion, interiors, and performance costume across a history spanning from the 16th century to the present.

## Overview

The harlequin pattern is composed of repeating diamonds (or elongated diamond shapes standing on point) in alternating contrasting colours or values. Its origin lies specifically in the costume of the Harlequin (Arlecchino) character from the Italian Commedia dell'arte tradition, whose distinctive patchwork of multi-coloured diamond or lozenge-shaped fabric became one of the most recognised costume motifs in European popular culture. The pattern is a subset of the broader [[Diamond Pattern]] and [[Check Pattern]] family, distinguished by its theatrical origin and its characteristic use of high contrast, multiple colours, or bold two-tone compositions.

## Visual Characteristics

The harlequin pattern uses a straight repeat of diamonds or lozenges (elongated diamonds standing on end) in alternating colour or tonal contrast. Each diamond unit is a distinct colour — the traditional harlequin uses a patchwork of many colours (red, blue, green, yellow, orange), while the commercial surface pattern version more often uses a bold two-tone or limited multi-colour palette for clarity and reproduction. The elongation of the diamond (taller than wide) creates the characteristic "standing on point" rhythm of the harlequin grid.

## Pattern Structure

The harlequin is an all-over repeat using a brick or offset square grid rotated 45 degrees — each diamond aligns to produce a continuous diagonal lattice. In full-colour traditional harlequin, each diamond is individually coloured from a palette of four to six or more colours with no two adjacent diamonds sharing the same colour. In commercial two-tone harlequin, diamonds alternate between two contrasting colours in a simple checkerboard-on-the-diagonal arrangement.

## Common Motifs

The harlequin motif is the diamond repeat itself — the pattern is structural rather than motif-based in the traditional sense. Design variation occurs through:
- Colour palette choice (traditional multicolour, duotone, monochromatic)
- Diamond proportion (square, elongated, short)
- Edge treatment (hard, clean edges vs. softened or irregular diamond outlines)
- Scale (small all-over micro-print to large-scale oversized diamond)
- Embellishment (additional small motifs placed within each diamond field)

## Colour and Style

Traditional harlequin uses a maximum-contrast multicolour palette — red, blue, yellow, green, and orange in no consistent arrangement, creating the "patchwork" effect of the Commedia costume. 20th-century fashion deployments (Adele Simpson, 1944; Oscar de la Renta, 2001) tend to use more resolved palettes — bold multicolour charmeuse in luxury fashion. Two-tone harlequin (black and white, navy and cream) is the most commercially versatile simplified version. Contemporary interior design applications often use two-tone or limited palette harlequin for sophistication.

## History and Context

The harlequin costume — the multicoloured diamond-patched suit of the Arlecchino character — originated in 16th-century Commedia dell'arte, the Italian improvised theatrical tradition. The costume's distinctive diamond patchwork pattern became a recognised motif in European decorative arts, appearing in paintings, ceramics, and textiles from the 17th century. In fashion, the pattern was popularised as a bold modern print by American designer Adele Simpson in 1944, whose prominent diamond design brought it into mid-century fashion. The Picasso retrospective of 1962 (Picasso frequently painted harlequin figures) fuelled a 1960s trend for harlequin-referencing fashion and interiors. Freddie Mercury wore notable harlequin print trousers in the late 1970s. Oscar de la Renta's 2001 multicoloured silk charmeuse gown is a documented haute couture example.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Fashion** — harlequin print is commercially active in womenswear and eveningwear; theatrical, maximalist, and vintage-referencing positioning; also active in children's fashion and costumes
- **Circus and performance textiles** — harlequin pattern is standard in performance costume, circus, and theatrical fabric
- **Wallpaper** — diamond harlequin wallpaper is an established category in heritage and theatrical interior design
- **Home textiles** — harlequin cushions and upholstery fabric are commercially available in bold heritage interiors contexts
- **Stationery and gifting** — harlequin diamond motifs appear in licensed print for giftwrap and cards
