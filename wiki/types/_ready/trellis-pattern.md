# Trellis Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/trellis-pattern/profile/trellis-pattern-image.svg)
A surface pattern based on the open lattice framework of a garden trellis — an interlocking geometric grid structure, traditionally used to support climbing plants, translated into decorative pattern for textiles and wallpaper.

## Overview

The trellis pattern draws its identity from the architectural garden structure of the same name: an open, interlocking grid of bamboo, timber, or metal that provides a framework for climbing foliage. Translated into surface pattern, the trellis becomes a geometric lattice motif — either used as a primary grid structure with foliage elements filling the cells, or simplified to a geometric linear pattern without botanical content. The pattern sits at the intersection of geometric structure and garden/botanical aesthetics, and is strongly associated with home furnishing rather than apparel.

## Visual Characteristics

Trellis patterns are defined by their lattice framework: an interlocking diagonal or orthogonal grid of intersecting lines or bands. The characteristic form is a repeating diamond or square cell structure. In botanical trellis patterns, climbing plants — roses, wisteria, jasmine, ivy, clematis — fill the cells, grow along the framework, or trail over and around the structure. In geometric trellis patterns (sometimes called latticework), the linear grid structure alone forms the pattern with no botanical content.

## Pattern Structure

The trellis pattern uses a regular, repeating grid structure — either a diagonal diamond grid or an orthogonal square/rectangular grid. Motifs placed within or along the grid lines tile predictably. The framework provides a strong structural armature that can accommodate a wide range of secondary motifs (flowers, leaves, birds, fruit) placed at intersections, within cells, or trailing along bars. In simplified geometric form, the trellis functions as a linear lattice repeat.

## Common Motifs

Primary trellis motifs:
- **Lattice bars:** the structural grid itself, rendered as flat bands or dimensional wooden/metal bars
- **Climbing plants:** roses, wisteria, jasmine, ivy, clematis, morning glory; the most traditional trellis botanical content
- **Intersection ornaments:** small decorative elements placed at the points where trellis bars cross

The source notes that Susan Meller's *Textile Designs* specifically situates trellis patterns in "home-furnishing fabrics rather than apparel" — a characterisation that holds broadly for the category.

## Colour and Style

Trellis patterns in their botanical form tend toward garden palettes: greens (foliage), whites and pinks (climbing roses, jasmine), mauves and purples (wisteria, clematis), and pale backgrounds suggesting rendered wall or sky. The geometric lattice version is more flexible — it appears in metallic (gold lattice on white or navy), classic blue-and-white Chinese porcelain traditions, and contemporary graphic reinterpretations. Ground colours range from white and cream (traditional) through to deep navy, forest green, and black (contemporary and maximalist).

## History and Context

William Morris designed his first trellis wallpaper in 1864 — widely considered the first wallpaper he designed. Produced while he was living at The Red House in Kent (the house built for him by Philip Webb), the "Trellis" design features a regular diamond trellis framework with roses and birds (reportedly drawn by Philip Webb). It established the trellis as a core motif of the Arts and Crafts movement and remains in commercial production as part of the Morris & Co. archive. The garden trellis as a domestic design reference connects interior pattern to the cultivated English garden aesthetic — a cultural connection that has sustained the trellis motif in British and broader Western home furnishing design since the Victorian period. Bamboo trellis structures additionally carry symbolic meaning in Asian design traditions, where bamboo represents harmony and balance.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Wallpaper:** the trellis is a historically prominent wallpaper motif, particularly in traditional and heritage interior styling
- **Home furnishing fabrics:** upholstery, curtains, and cushion fabric — the primary market for botanical trellis patterns per the source
- **Bedding:** trellis and lattice patterns appear in bedding collections, particularly in garden-adjacent aesthetics
- **Tile design:** geometric trellis/lattice patterns translate directly to ceramic tile designs for bathrooms and kitchens
