# Dog Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/dog-pattern/profile/dog-pattern-image.svg)
A surface pattern category featuring dogs as primary motifs — drawing on a long tradition of dog depiction in decorative arts, heraldry, portraiture, and popular culture to produce commercially active designs across gifting, home textiles, fashion accessories, and stationery.

## Overview

Dog pattern draws on one of the oldest and most widespread human-animal relationships. Dogs have been depicted in decorative arts across cultures and centuries — from prehistoric rock carvings and Greek ceramics through Renaissance portraiture and Victorian oil painting to contemporary character illustration. As a surface pattern motif, dog imagery carries strong associations with loyalty, companionship, and domestic life, making it commercially reliable across gifting, home textiles, and lifestyle products. The variety of dog breeds provides a visually rich design vocabulary — from the elegant silhouette of the greyhound through the square-headed bulldog to the fluffy outline of the samoyed.

## Visual Characteristics

Dog patterns vary widely by style and intended market: realistic breed portraits; stylised or cartoon character illustration; silhouette and outline forms; and abstract breed-pattern references (hound's-tooth, a check derived from herringbone weave). Heraldic dog depictions use specific formal conventions — greyhounds are shown courant (running), mastiffs and talbot hounds are shown passant (walking). Victorian dog portraiture established a tradition of high-detail oil-paint-style breed illustration that continues as a surface pattern reference. Japanese netsuke and Edo-period objects contributed carved dog motifs to the decorative arts vocabulary.

## Pattern Structure

Dog patterns commonly appear as:
- **All-over breed repeat** — multiple breeds or a single breed repeated across the surface in varied poses
- **Portrait scatter** — individual dog portraits distributed as a scattered all-over repeat
- **Silhouette repeat** — solid or outlined dog silhouettes used as a graphic all-over pattern
- **Single motif** — a single dog portrait or breed used as a centred or panel motif on homeware, accessories, or stationery
- **Heraldic composition** — stylised heraldic dog forms used in formal repeat or border compositions

## Common Motifs

- Greyhound (the most heraldically significant and graphically elegant dog silhouette)
- Spaniel, dachshund, poodle, labrador, bulldog, terrier (popular commercial breed motifs)
- Dog portraits — specific breed representations in illustration or painterly style
- Dog bone, paw print, and collar as companion motifs
- Hunting dog scenes — dogs with riders, horses, and game; associated with equestrian and country aesthetics
- Jan van Eyck's Arnolfini dog — a specific cultural reference for dog as fidelity symbol

## Colour and Style

Dog patterns use any colourway appropriate to the market application. Realistic breed illustration uses the animal's natural coat colours. Heraldic and historical treatments use the formal palette of heraldry — gold, red, blue, black, and silver on white or coloured grounds. Contemporary character illustration uses bold, graphic colour combinations. Earthy and muted tones are associated with country/equestrian dog pattern positioning; bright saturated colours with children's and novelty applications.

## History and Context

Dog depictions appear in rock shelter paintings approximately 9,000 years old (Bhimbetka, India). Bronze Age tomb decorations include dog imagery. Greek and Roman ceramics, reliefs, and mosaics frequently depict dogs — the Cave Canem (Beware of the Dog) mosaic is one of the most famous examples from Pompeii. In heraldry, specific dog breeds carry encoded meanings: the greyhound signifies speed and loyalty; the talbot hound signifies fidelity and courage; mastiffs and bloodhounds signify strength and vigilance. Jan van Eyck's Arnolfini Portrait (1434) includes a dog as a symbol of fidelity — a canonical art history example of dog symbolism in Western painting. Edwin Landseer established Victorian dog portraiture as a commercially significant genre. The founding of the Kennel Club UK (1873) and American Kennel Club (1884) formalised breed standards that subsequently drove demand for breed-specific depiction in art and decorative goods. Japanese Edo-period netsuke (1615–1868) include carved dog forms as functional decorative objects.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Gifting** — dog-themed gifts are a major commercial category; pattern appears on mugs, cushions, bags, clothing, and stationery for dog owners
- **Home textiles** — dog pattern cushions, throws, and towels are commercially active in pet-owner home decor
- **Fashion accessories** — dog print scarves, bags, and socks are consistent commercial categories
- **Stationery** — dog illustration on notecards, notebooks, and desk accessories is commercially reliable
- **Equestrian and country market** — hunting dog scene prints are commercially active in country lifestyle and equestrian homeware
