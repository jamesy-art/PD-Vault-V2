# Dragon Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/dragon-pattern/profile/dragon-pattern-image.svg)
A surface pattern category featuring dragon motifs — drawing primarily from Chinese, Japanese, and Korean dragon iconography for surface design applications in fashion, wallpaper, fabric, and homeware, with strong historical roots in imperial textile traditions.

## Overview

Dragon pattern in surface design is dominated by East Asian iconographic traditions — particularly the Chinese imperial dragon with its coiled body, five claws, and accompanying symbolic elements (flaming pearl, clouds, waves). This tradition connects to one of the most historically significant applications of surface pattern design: the ceremonial robes of Chinese, Korean, and Japanese imperial courts, in which dragon motifs were encoded symbols of rank and political authority. In contemporary surface design, dragon pattern carries associations with power, mythology, and Asian cultural heritage, and is commercially active across fashion, wallpaper, licensing, and gift products.

## Visual Characteristics

The Chinese dragon form is characterised by a long winding serpentine body, large scaled mane, prominent antler-like horns, and clawed feet. Unlike Western dragons, Chinese dragons typically have no wings — their ability to fly is inherent. The five-clawed dragon is an imperial emblem; four-clawed variants were used for lower nobility; three-clawed dragons appear in Japanese tradition. Accompanying motifs — flaming pearls, stylised clouds, ocean waves, and fire — are integral components of the dragon composition, filling the space around the dragon form and giving the composition its characteristic dynamism and density.

## Pattern Structure

Dragon patterns in historical textile applications use large-scale positioned motifs rather than small all-over repeats:
- **Frontal facing dragon** — a single large dragon viewed face-on, placed centrally on a robe; used in Chinese imperial court dress
- **Coiled dragon composition** — a dragon shown in a coiled or curved pose contained within a circular or medallion field; used in Chinese and Korean court textiles
- **All-over dragon repeat** — in modern commercial applications, smaller dragon forms are repeated across the surface as an all-over pattern
- **Border and facing compositions** — dragons placed in repeat along borders and dress facings; standard in historical robe construction
- **Japanese Uroko pattern** — a separate Japanese tradition of triangle/scale repeat patterns associated with dragon scales

## Common Motifs

- Chinese imperial five-clawed dragon (long)
- Dragon with flaming pearl — the pearl represents wisdom, power, and spiritual truth
- Stylised cloud forms accompanying dragon compositions
- Ocean wave motifs (particularly in Chinese and Japanese dragon contexts)
- Japanese dragon with natural landscape elements (water, clouds, mist)
- Korean dragon robe (용포) compositions
- Dragon scale pattern (Uroko) — the geometric triangular scale texture abstracted from dragon skin
- Flames and fire accompanying Western dragon or modern commercial dragon forms
- Ouroboros — a dragon biting its own tail; a circular symbol of infinity and cyclicality (cross-cultural)

## Colour and Style

Chinese imperial dragon robes used a specific set of regulated colours. Yellow was reserved for the emperor; other ranked persons used specific colours including blue, red, and brown. Gold thread embroidery was standard in high-status court textiles. Contemporary surface design dragon pattern uses any colourway — black and gold for luxury/editorial positioning; red and gold for Chinese cultural celebration contexts; jewel tones for fashion applications; graphic black-and-white for tattoo-adjacent design work.

## History and Context

The Chinese imperial dragon has been an emblem of the emperor since at least the Han dynasty (206 BC – 220 AD). Post-Tang dynasty emperors restricted dragon imagery to imperial use on clothing and articles. Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1912) dynasty ceremonial robes — intricate embroidered garments — are among the most elaborate textile applications of dragon pattern in history, with the number of dragon claws explicitly regulated by rank. In Korea, the dragon robe (용포) used a five-taloned dragon for the king and four-taloned for the crown prince — a direct parallel to the Chinese system. In Japan, dragons are depicted as three-clawed water deities. The Mesopotamian mušḫuššu (a composite dragon-like creature) represents an entirely separate iconographic tradition. The ouroboros — a dragon or serpent eating its own tail — appears across Egyptian, Greek, and alchemical traditions. Modern commercial applications of dragon pattern span surface design for fabrics, wallpapers, wrapping paper, apparel graphics, and packaging.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Fashion textiles** — dragon print is commercially active in luxury womenswear, eveningwear, and streetwear; Chinese New Year seasonal collections drive specific commercial demand
- **Wallpaper** — dragon pattern wallpaper is an established category in chinoiserie and Asian-inspired interior design
- **Licensing** — dragon motifs are commercially licensable across fashion accessories, homeware, and gift products
- **Fabric and apparel** — dragon pattern is commercially available on POD platforms and in fashion fabric wholesale
- **Commercial graphics** — dragons appear as logos, apparel graphics, and branding for energy, gaming, and performance-oriented commercial clients
