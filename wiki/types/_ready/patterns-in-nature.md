# Patterns in nature

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/patterns-in-nature/profile/patterns-in-nature-image.svg)
The recurring visual structures found in the natural world — from plant spirals and animal markings to wave forms and crystal cracks — that serve as primary source material and conceptual inspiration for surface pattern design.

## Overview

Natural patterns are regular structures produced by physical, biological, and mathematical processes operating in living organisms and non-living physical systems. For surface pattern designers, patterns in nature are among the most important reference sources — providing the visual vocabulary of organic geometry, the colour relationships of natural pigmentation, and the structural logic of systems that repeat at multiple scales. The recognition that all natural patterns arise from a small set of underlying mechanisms — symmetry, reaction-diffusion, Fibonacci sequence, fractal iteration — gives surface designers a conceptual framework for generating and analysing naturally-inspired surface design work.

## Visual Characteristics

Natural patterns range from highly regular and geometric (honeycomb, crystal lattice, radiolarian) to apparently organic and irregular (leaf venation, river deltas, animal coat markings). The key insight from mathematical pattern analysis is that apparently complex organic patterns are typically generated by simple iterative rules — the Fibonacci spiral seen in sunflower seed heads, nautilus shells, and pine cone scales follows a single mathematical principle. Surface designs that successfully reference natural patterns typically capture the underlying logic (the proportion, the density, the repeat structure) rather than simply copying a surface appearance.

## Pattern Structure

Natural pattern categories identified in the source, each with direct surface design relevance:

- **Symmetry** — bilateral symmetry (butterfly wings, leaf forms), radial symmetry (flowers, starfish, radiolaria); the most directly applicable to surface design composition
- **Fractal / tree structure** — self-similar branching patterns (trees, river deltas, lightning, leaf venation); the Mandelbrot set (1975) formalised fractal geometry; Sierpinski triangle and Koch snowflake are two-dimensional fractal examples relevant to surface design
- **Spiral** — Fibonacci sequence and golden ratio spirals appear in phyllotaxis (sunflower seeds, pine cones, nautilus), shell growth, and galaxy form; 17 wallpaper groups of tilings govern all possible plane-filling spiral-based tilings
- **Chaos and meander** — irregular but self-organising structures; river meanders, the fractal patterns of coastlines
- **Waves and dunes** — parallel or undulating forms produced by wind or water on sand, water, or ice surfaces
- **Foam and bubbles** — Plateau's laws govern the geometry of soap bubble interfaces (three-edge junctions at 120°, four-edge junctions in three dimensions); honeycomb is the two-dimensional cellular equivalent
- **Tessellation** — space-filling tile patterns; honeycomb (hexagonal) and snake's head fritillary petal arrangements are biological examples; relevant to all geometric tiling surface design
- **Cracks** — irregular polygonal crack patterns in drying clay, cooling lava (columnar jointing), and craquelure in old glazed ceramics; visually referenced in surface design for organic texture
- **Spots and stripes** — animal coat markings (leopard spots, zebra stripes, tabby cat markings) explained by Alan Turing's reaction-diffusion model (1952); the same mathematical mechanism generates both spots and stripes depending on parameter values

## Common Motifs

Natural patterns that have direct and documented surface design application include:
- **Fibonacci spiral / phyllotaxis** — sunflower, pine cone, nautilus; circular spiral compositions
- **Honeycomb / hexagonal tessellation** — a core geometric pattern derived from natural bee construction
- **Snowflake / crystalline symmetry** — sixfold rotational symmetry; referenced in geometric and winter pattern design
- **Animal coat patterns** — leopard rosettes, zebra stripes, tiger stripes, giraffe patches; the primary source for animal print surface design
- **Leaf venation** — branching dendritic structure; referenced in botanical illustration and organic abstract design
- **Wave form** — undulating line repeat derived from wave surface patterns
- **Wood grain** — parallel curved line patterns in timber; referenced in wood-effect surface print

## Colour and Style

Natural colour is not addressed as a structural category in this entry — it is covered in the individual colour-group type pages. The structural patterns of nature are colour-independent: they describe how forms are organised, not what colours they are.

## History and Context

The study of pattern in nature connects mathematical science to design practice. Key historical figures in this tradition:
- **Pythagoras** — mathematical proportion and harmony in natural forms
- **Plato** — the doctrine of Forms as underlying ideal patterns in nature
- **Leonardo da Vinci** — systematic observation and drawing of natural pattern (water flow, plant growth, geological formation)
- **Fibonacci** (Leonardo Bonacci, c.1170–1250) — the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) introduced to Europe in 1202; governs plant phyllotaxis
- **Ernst Haeckel** (1834–1919) — painted and catalogued marine organisms, revealing the extraordinary geometric complexity of radiolaria, jellyfish, and other microscopic life; his illustrations directly influenced Art Nouveau surface design
- **D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson** — On Growth and Form (1917); the foundational scientific analysis of mathematical pattern in biological growth and form
- **Alan Turing** — mathematical model of morphogenesis (1952); the reaction-diffusion mechanism that explains how spots and stripes form in animal coats; a key theoretical tool for understanding natural surface pattern generation
- **Benoit Mandelbrot** — fractal geometry (1975); formal mathematical description of self-similar natural patterns at multiple scales

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Botanical and floral design** — phyllotaxis, spiral, and plant symmetry structures directly inform botanical pattern composition
- **Animal print** — all animal coat patterns (leopard, zebra, tiger, giraffe) are natural patterns with direct surface design translation
- **Geometric pattern** — honeycomb, crystalline symmetry, and other natural tilings are direct sources for geometric surface design
- **Organic abstract** — natural crack, foam, and wave patterns are referenced in contemporary organic abstract surface design
- **Nature-inspired pattern generally** — patterns in nature are the primary non-human reference source for surface pattern design across all market categories
