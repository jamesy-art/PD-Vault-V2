# Green Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/green-pattern/profile/green-pattern-image.svg)
A colour reference page for the green spectrum in surface pattern design — covering shade vocabulary, symbolic associations, and commercial applications of green in textile and print design.

## Overview

Green is one of the most commercially significant colours in surface pattern design, with strong cultural associations with nature, growth, and renewal that make it a perennial in botanical, floral, and nature-inspired pattern. The green spectrum is exceptionally broad — from the yellow-green of chartreuse and lime through the blue-green of seafoam, jade, and emerald, to the grey-green of sage, olive, and moss. Each shade carries distinct commercial and cultural associations relevant to pattern positioning and market application.

## Visual Characteristics

Green in surface pattern spans a wide tonal range. The design industry uses specific shade names as shorthand for aesthetic positioning: sage and olive signal natural/earthy/muted aesthetics; emerald and jade signal luxury and richness; lime and neon green signal energy and contemporary pop; mint and pastel green signal softness and freshness; forest and hunter greens signal heritage and tradition. The Pantone system documents specific green standards; Pantone 3507 is designated as neon green (also referenced as Charli XCX "Brat" green in 2024 cultural context).

## Pattern Structure

Green functions as a colour applied to any pattern structure — it does not define or constrain the structural type. The notes in this entry focus on green as a colour identity and palette resource. Green-dominant patterns are most strongly associated with botanical, floral, and nature-inspired designs where green functions as the ground, foliage, or field colour.

## Common Motifs

Green is most naturally associated with foliage, plant stems, botanical illustration, and nature-derived surface pattern. Key surface design contexts where specific green shades dominate:
- **Sage green** — botanical, natural, slow-living interiors aesthetic; widely commercially used in contemporary home textiles
- **Olive green** — earthy, utilitarian, outdoors associations; active in fashion and homeware
- **Emerald green** — jewel-toned luxury; interior design and fashion
- **Mint green** — fresh, soft, nostalgic; confectionery, children's, and vintage aesthetic
- **Celadon** — Chinese ceramic tradition; soft grey-green associated with glazed porcelain; important in East Asian-influenced surface design
- **Kelly green** — bold, sports-adjacent; Irish cultural associations
- **Forest and hunter green** — heritage, outdoor, traditional British aesthetic

## Colour and Style

Documented named green shades with design relevance:

- Sage, olive, army, apple, moss, avocado — earthy, muted register
- Chartreuse, grass, neon, lime — yellow-green, vivid register
- Tea green, mint, pastel green — soft, light register
- Kelly green, emerald, jade, seafoam — saturated, rich register
- Pine, forest, hunter — deep, dark register

Historically significant greens:
- **Celadon** — derived from Chinese ceramics; soft grey-green of glazed porcelain; named in English from Chinese ceramic tradition
- **Hooker's green** — named after botanical artist William Hooker (1779–1832); historically used in botanical illustration
- **Persian green** — associated with Persian pottery and carpet design; c. 1892 English-language naming
- **Emerald green** — synthetic pigment invented in Germany in 1814
- **Paris Green / Scheele's Green** — Victorian-era arsenic-based pigment; historically significant but toxic; responsible for numerous Victorian-era poisoning cases via dyed wallpaper
- **Neon green** — Pantone 3507; gained cultural prominence as Charli XCX "Brat" green in 2024

## History and Context

Green pigments have had a complex history in decorative arts and textiles. The Victorian arsenic-based Paris Green was widely used for wallpaper, fabric, and artificial flowers before its toxicity was understood — William Morris initially used it in his wallpaper production. Natural green dyes (weld, woad combined with weld, plant-based mordant dyeing) preceded synthetic alternatives. The development of synthetic green pigments from the 19th century made consistent, stable green reproduction possible in both print and textile applications. Contemporary green shade cycling in interiors is well-documented: sage green has been commercially dominant in the 2020s homeware market; forest green and emerald have strong contemporary interior design presence.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Botanical and floral design** — green is the dominant foliage colour in floral and botanical surface pattern; sage and forest greens are commercially dominant in contemporary botanical wallpaper and fabric
- **Nature-inspired textiles** — leaf prints, tropical patterns, and botanical surface designs rely on the full green spectrum
- **Wallpaper** — green in all registers is commercially active in residential wallpaper; sage, forest, and dark green wallpapers have been leading commercial colours in the 2020s
- **Fashion** — olive, sage, and emerald are commercially active in seasonal womenswear; army green has sustained menswear presence
- **Licensing** — green-palette surface pattern is commercially active in stationery, gifting, and homeware licensing categories
