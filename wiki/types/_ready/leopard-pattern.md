# Leopard Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/leopard-pattern/profile/leopard-pattern-image.svg)
An animal print surface pattern replicating the spotted coat of the leopard — one of the most commercially durable animal print categories in fashion, with black rosette spots on a gold or tan ground as the canonical form.

## Overview

Leopard pattern replicates the coat markings of the leopard (Panthera pardus) — black or dark brown spots and rosettes on a tawny gold to tan ground. It is the dominant animal print pattern type in fashion, with a commercial history in womenswear extending from early 20th-century fur substitution through to a continuous contemporary presence. Leopard print sits within the broader [[Animal Print]] category and is specifically distinguished from [[Cheetah Pattern]] (which has solid black spots on a yellow ground, without rosettes) by its characteristic rosette structure: irregularly shaped clusters of spots forming partial ring shapes around a lighter centre.

## Visual Characteristics

The leopard's coat pattern is characterised by irregular rosettes — clusters of dark spots arranged in a roughly circular or oval formation around a slightly darker centre area (lighter than the spots themselves but darker than the open coat ground). The rosettes are distributed irregularly across the tawny golden-yellow to tan ground, with the overall surface reading as a densely spotted field. In commercial fabric and surface design, the leopard print is often simplified and regularised — the rosette shapes become more consistent and the overall repeat is more structured than the natural coat. Colour range varies: the classic form uses black spots on amber/gold; fashion interpretations use any colour combination while maintaining the rosette structure.

## Pattern Structure

In its natural form, the leopard coat has no strict repeat — rosettes vary in size and density across different body areas. Commercial leopard print translates this into a semi-random all-over repeat in which rosettes are distributed at irregular intervals and rotations to simulate the organic distribution of the natural pattern while maintaining printable repeat structure. Leopard print is typically an all-over tossed repeat — the surface is fully covered with rosette and spot elements with no visible ground repeat.

## Common Motifs

- Leopard rosette — the defining motif; irregular dark-ring/cluster form around a slightly lighter centre
- Open spots — smaller solid dark spots interspersed between rosettes (as in the natural coat)
- Other spotted coat references in the same family:
  - Snow leopard — larger, more open rosettes with grey-and-white palette; softer aesthetic
  - Clouded leopard — large irregular cloud-shaped spots in dark brown on paler ground
  - Jaguar — similar rosette structure to leopard but with a distinctive black dot inside each rosette

## Colour and Style

The classic leopard print uses black or near-black rosettes on a golden amber or tan ground — a warm, rich colour combination strongly associated with exotic luxury in fashion. Commercial fashion interpretations have expanded the palette across all registers:
- Monochrome leopard (black/dark grey spots on white or grey ground) — a more graphic, contemporary treatment
- Coloured leopard (rosettes in any colour, on any ground) — used in trend-forward fashion and accessories
- Neon leopard — ultra-bright colour combinations; trend-specific
- Tonal or tone-on-tone leopard — spots and ground in close-value shades; subtle luxury

## History and Context

Leopard and other big cat pelts were luxury status goods in ancient cultures — worn by Egyptian pharaohs, Roman emperors, and African royalty. The use of leopard fur in Western fashion is documented from the early 20th century. From the mid-20th century, concern about the conservation impact of using real fur drove the shift to faux fur and printed fabric leopard pattern as a fashion category. Leopard print in womenswear became associated with a bold, confident, and overtly sexual fashion aesthetic in the 1950s–1960s and has remained commercially active as an enduring "power print" in fashion, with regular major revivals. The pattern's commercial resilience in fashion over a 70+ year period makes it unusual among animal prints.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Fashion** — leopard print is among the highest-volume and most commercially durable animal prints in womenswear; commercially active in tops, dresses, skirts, coats, and accessories year-round with regular trend peaks
- **Fashion accessories** — scarves, bags, shoes, and belts in leopard print are a continuous commercial category
- **Home textiles** — leopard print cushions, throws, and upholstery fabric are commercially active in maximalist and glamour-aesthetic interiors
- **Licensing** — leopard print motifs are commercially licensable across fashion accessories, stationery, and homeware
