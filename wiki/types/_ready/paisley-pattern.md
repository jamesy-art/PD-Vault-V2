# Paisley Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/paisley-pattern/profile/paisley-pattern-image.svg)
A teardrop-shaped curved motif with a distinctive bent tip — derived from the Iranian/Persian boteh/buta, transported to Europe via Kashmir shawls, and one of the most commercially durable pattern types in fashion and textiles.

## Overview

Paisley is a curved, teardrop-shaped motif with a characteristic bent or curved upper end. The motif originated in Iranian and Persian decorative art — where it is called boteh or buta — and entered the European market through the importation of Kashmir shawls from the 17th century onwards. Its English name comes from Paisley, the Scottish town whose Jacquard-loom weavers became the dominant European producers of paisley-pattern fabric in the 19th century. Paisley is one of the most commercially enduring pattern types in Western fashion, with documented presence from early 19th-century bandanas through to continuous contemporary production. It is known by different names in different cultures: "mango" in most Indian and South Asian languages; "cucumbers" in Russia; "ham hock pattern" in China; "boteh," "cachemire," or "palme" in French design vocabulary.

## Visual Characteristics

The paisley motif is defined by its teardrop-shaped body with a curved or bent upper end — the characteristic curving tip that distinguishes it from a plain teardrop or droplet form. The interior of the motif is densely filled with subsidiary ornamental elements: smaller floral forms, leaves, dots, fine scrollwork, and geometric fill. The motif is typically surrounded by secondary fill elements. The overall effect is richly ornamental — paisley compositions are dense, fine-detailed, and complex. Scale varies from very small (micro paisley for scarves and accessories) to large (bold oversized paisleys for fashion print).

## Pattern Structure

Paisley is most commonly used in all-over repeat format — individual or clustered paisley motifs distributed across the surface in tossed, half-drop, or directional arrangements. A characteristic traditional paisley composition uses a central large boteh motif with the curved tip pointing in one direction (the "mango" or "cone" form), repeated across the surface with fill ornament between. Paisley is also used as: a border strip (narrow repeating paisley along a hem or edge); a contained medallion composition; and a single large placement motif.

## Common Motifs

- Boteh/buta — the core teardrop-with-curved-tip form; the defining element of all paisley
- Interior floral fill — small flowers, leaves, and scroll forms within the boteh body
- Exterior fill — fine dot, floral, or scrollwork elements surrounding each motif
- Millefleurs-style fill (very dense all-over subsidiary floral)
- Bandana-associated compositions (often simplified paisley in red, navy, or black on contrast ground)
- Mughal floral and vine fill elements (in traditional Kashmir and Indian paisley)
- Modern graphic paisley (simplified, bold, flat-colour treatment of the boteh form)

## Colour and Style

Traditional Kashmir and Indian paisley uses rich, saturated colour — deep reds, blues, greens, and golds against cream or contrasting grounds. European 19th-century Paisley and Norwich production evolved the palette within these conventions. Bandana paisley (from the early 1800s) uses the characteristic red/navy-on-white or white-on-red/navy palette that remains commercially dominant for bandana products. 1960s fashion revival (Beatles era) used bold, psychedelic paisley palettes. Contemporary commercial paisley spans everything from heritage-exact Kashmir reproduction to vivid fashion colour to monochromatic single-colour treatments.

## History and Context

The earliest documented appearance of the boteh motif in decorative art is at the Noh Gumba mosque in Balkh, Afghanistan, dated to the 800s. The motif moved from Iran to Kashmir approximately in the 1400s, becoming established in the Kashmir shawl weaving tradition. The Kashmir shawl — woven in fine goat-fibre wool using complex twill weave and twill-tapestry techniques — became a major luxury export good, reaching European aristocratic consumers through the British and Dutch East India Companies. The Mughal Emperor Akbar wore two Kashmir shawls simultaneously as a status symbol, establishing the shawl's prestige association. European commercial interest drove the development of Jacquard-loom weaving in Western textile centres — Paisley, Scotland became the dominant production centre, reaching five-colour production capability by 1820 and fifteen colours by 1860. Bandana paisley production began in the early 1800s. The pattern's commercial peak in Western fashion was approximately 1800–1870. The 1960s Beatles-era revival, and subsequent associations with Prince (whose record label and studio complex was named Paisley Park), Queen, and David Bowie, gave the pattern a rock-music cultural register that continues in commercial use. Today paisley is commercially active in neckties, waistcoats, scarves, womenswear, wallpaper, cushions, and bedding.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Fashion accessories** — neckties and scarves are the dominant contemporary commercial format for paisley pattern; bandanas remain a high-volume commercial product
- **Womenswear** — paisley print is commercially active in skirts, blouses, and dresses, particularly in bohemian, Indian-inspired, and heritage-aesthetic positioning
- **Wallpaper and home textiles** — paisley wallpaper, cushion fabric, and bedding are commercially active in heritage, maximalist, and global-inspired interiors
- **Licensing** — paisley is one of the most commercially licensable heritage pattern types, spanning fashion accessories, stationery, homeware, and gifting
