# Gradation Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/gradation-pattern/profile/gradation-pattern-image.svg)
Surface pattern structure and design technique where one or more elements — most commonly colour, but also scale, shape, or direction — shift progressively from one state to another across the composition. Creates depth, movement, and rhythm through continuous change rather than abrupt contrast.

## Overview

Gradation describes the systematic progression of a design element across a surface. In surface pattern design, it appears primarily as colour gradation — the ombré or gradient effect where a hue transitions smoothly from light to dark, or one colour flows into another. Scale gradation (motifs progressively shrinking or enlarging), shape gradation (motifs morphing from one form to another), and directional gradation (angles incrementally shifting) are less common but equally valid applications. Gradation is a structural principle rather than a specific visual style: it applies across abstract, geometric, and figurative pattern categories.

## Visual Characteristics

Gradation is characterised by the absence of hard transitions. In colour gradation, the shift from one state to another reads as smooth and continuous — a gradient wash rather than a striped sequence. Step-based gradation produces a more graphic staircase quality. Scale gradation creates a sense of depth or perspective: motifs that diminish toward one side of the surface suggest recession; motifs that enlarge suggest emergence. Directional gradation — where repeating lines or elements incrementally angle across the composition — creates a sense of twist, flow, or rotation.

## Pattern Structure

- **Gradient/ombré field**: a colour transition applied as a ground wash, often combined with a secondary motif repeat; the gradient itself is the primary structural element
- **Progressive-repeat**: motif size or spacing increases or decreases systematically across the tile, so the overall composition shifts from dense to open or small to large
- **Fading repeat**: an all-over pattern that diminishes to the ground colour across the tile, creating a receding impression
- **Shape-morph sequence**: a motif sequence in which each unit transitions slightly toward the next form — suitable for small-run or placement prints rather than seamless all-overs

## Common Motifs

- Gradient colour field (standalone or as ground for a secondary motif)
- Progressive scale scatter (same motif, systematically varied in size)
- Fading all-over (standard repeat that dissolves to ground)
- Morphing shape sequence (placement print application)

## Colour and Style

- **Monochrome ombré**: tonal shift within a single hue — the most commercially active form; clean, elegant, widely applied
- **Analogous colour gradient**: shift through adjacent hues (blue to purple, yellow to orange) — warm, painterly
- **Complementary gradient**: shift between opposing hues — visually arresting, more editorial in use
- **Multi-colour gradient**: complex hue shifts through several colours — strong in contemporary fashion and activewear

Paul Klee's *Crystal Gradation* (1921) — a step-based colour gradation in watercolour — is a widely cited fine art reference for the structured gradation aesthetic.

## History and Context

Gradation as a visual arts technique — the deliberate transition from one hue or tone to another to create spatial depth, atmosphere, and form — is a foundational principle documented across media from pastel and oil painting to printmaking (*The Encyclopedia of Pastel Techniques*, Judy Martin, 1992).

Halftone photography, developed in the late 19th century, mechanised gradation reproduction for printed media. Halftone screens — continuous patterns of small dots — allowed photographs to be reproduced with graduated tone in newspapers and magazines, a significant technical milestone enabling printed gradation at commercial scale.

Digital software tools made complex colour gradients widely accessible from the 1990s onward, substantially expanding the use of gradation effects in surface pattern design. The ombré dye effect has recurred across multiple fashion cycles as a hand-craft and printed textile aesthetic.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- Apparel: ombré dye effects, gradient print fabrics, activewear, swimwear, scarves
- Home textiles: gradient cushions, throws, curtains, bedding
- Wallpaper: colour wash and progressive repeat applications
- Stationery and packaging: gradient backgrounds, branded colour systems
- Digital surface licensing: gradient-based patterns are highly active in POD/digital product markets
