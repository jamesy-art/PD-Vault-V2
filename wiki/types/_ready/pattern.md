# Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/pattern/profile/pattern-image.svg)
The fundamental concept underlying all surface pattern design — any regular, predictable repetition of elements in the world, in design, or in mathematics; in decorative arts, specifically a repeating ornamental design applied to a surface.

## Overview

A pattern is any regular, predictable arrangement in which elements repeat according to a consistent rule. In surface pattern design, "pattern" refers specifically to a decorative design applied to a two-dimensional surface through repeating visual elements — the primary working material of the surface pattern designer. The concept unifies highly diverse visual territory: a geometric wallpaper grid, a botanical print repeat, and a houndstooth check are all patterns in the full sense — each is governed by translational symmetry and a defined repeat unit. The word "pattern" in the decorative arts context specifically designates repeating ornamental designs manufactured for surfaces — ceramics, textiles, and wallpaper are the canonical application contexts.

## Visual Characteristics

Patterns are characterised by visual predictability and regularity — the eye can extrapolate what comes next from what it has already seen. This regularity can be strict (mathematically precise geometric tiling) or organic (naturalistic botanical repeats where the repeat logic is present but the motif variation makes it less immediately obvious). Patterns range from minimal and structural (a simple two-colour stripe) to maximally complex (a dense Mughal textile with hundreds of individual motifs in a single repeat unit). All patterns share the property of translational symmetry — the pattern looks the same when shifted by the repeat unit.

## Pattern Structure

Mathematical pattern analysis identifies several fundamental structural categories relevant to surface design:

- **Translational symmetry** — the pattern repeats in at least one direction; all repeating patterns have this
- **Bilateral symmetry** — the pattern is mirrored across a vertical or horizontal axis; common in floral and botanical design
- **Radial symmetry** — the pattern radiates from a central point; used in medallion and mandala compositions
- **Fivefold symmetry** — found in natural forms (sea urchins, starfish) and some geometric ornament
- **Wallpaper groups** — the 17 distinct symmetry groups that classify all possible repeating two-dimensional patterns; every surface pattern belongs to one of these 17 groups

Natural patterns documented in the source include: symmetries, spirals (nautilus, phyllotaxis, sunflower), meanders, waves, foams (subject to Plateau's laws governing bubble structure), tilings, cracks, and stripes.

## Common Motifs

Pattern as a concept encompasses all motif categories. In the context of the decorative arts specifically, the following are noted as canonical examples:
- William Morris patterns — Pimpernel, Strawberry Thief; cited as canonical examples of repeating decorative surface pattern
- Geometric patterns — "wallpaper design" is explicitly cited as the canonical example of geometric pattern in the source
- Natural pattern references — symmetry, spirals, meanders, waves, foams, tilings, stripes, cracks

## Colour and Style

As a meta-category, "pattern" as a concept is colour-neutral — any colour can be used in any pattern structure. Colour choice, motif scale, and repeat density together determine the commercial character and market positioning of a specific pattern.

## History and Context

Pattern recognition is a fundamental cognitive capacity — humans identify patterns in natural phenomena, social behaviour, and abstract systems. In mathematics, the study of pattern includes geometry, number theory, and chaos theory. In the natural world, patterns arise from physical processes: the Fibonacci sequence governs phyllotaxis (the spiral arrangement of seeds and petals); Turing's reaction-diffusion model (1952) explains spot and stripe patterns in animal coats; fractal geometry (Mandelbrot, 1975) describes the self-similar patterns of coastlines, trees, and snowflakes. In decorative arts, pattern-making is one of the oldest human activities — pattern has been used in ceramics, textiles, and building ornament across every culture. The term "pattern" in the decorative arts specifically designates repeating ornamental designs applied to manufactured surfaces.
