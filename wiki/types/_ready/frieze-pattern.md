# Frieze Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/frieze-pattern/profile/frieze-pattern-image.svg)
A border or strip pattern running continuously in a single horizontal direction — one of the seven symmetry classes of one-dimensional repeating design, with roots in classical architecture and a broad application in wallpaper borders, textile trims, and tile design.

## Overview

A frieze pattern is any decorative design that repeats continuously along a single axis, forming a horizontal band or strip. The term comes from classical architecture, where a frieze is the continuous horizontal band between the architrave and cornice of a building facade — typically ornamented with continuous sculptural or painted decoration. In surface pattern design and mathematics, "frieze" refers to the formal class of one-dimensional repeating patterns: designs with translational symmetry in exactly one direction. There are exactly seven distinct frieze symmetry groups (the "seven frieze groups"), making this a well-defined structural category with direct relevance to border pattern design.

## Visual Characteristics

Frieze patterns are defined by their linear, horizontal composition. Visually, they read as a band or strip of repeating ornament — a border rather than a field. Width is limited; length is infinite (or appears so). Classical frieze patterns use figural or sculptural ornament: running animals, human figures, acanthus scrolls, egg-and-dart moulding, and meander/key patterns. In surface design, frieze patterns can use any motif type — geometric, floral, figurative — provided they are organised in a one-directional repeat strip.

## Pattern Structure

The seven mathematically distinct frieze symmetry groups define all possible structural variations of a frieze pattern:

- **p111** — translation only; motif simply repeats in one direction
- **p1m1** — translation plus horizontal reflection (bilateral symmetry across the length of the strip)
- **pm11** — translation plus vertical reflection (mirror symmetry within each repeat unit)
- **pmm2** — translation plus both horizontal and vertical reflections
- **p112** — translation plus 180° rotation
- **p1a1** — translation plus glide reflection (motif flips and steps forward)
- **pma2** — translation plus glide reflection and vertical reflection

Understanding which symmetry group a border pattern belongs to allows pattern designers to verify the structural completeness and consistency of the repeat.

## Common Motifs

- Greek key / meander — the straight-line labyrinthine meander is the canonical frieze motif from the Greek geometric tradition
- Running scrolls and wave patterns (rounded versions of the meander)
- Egg-and-dart moulding — classical architectural ornament adapted into border design
- Acanthus and foliate scroll — continuous leaf-and-stem running ornament
- Geometric interlace — repeating angular or curved knotwork in a strip format
- Figurative processional — repeated animal or human figures in a running strip (as in Assyrian and Egyptian relief friezes)
- Navajo-style geometric strip patterns — angular geometric repeats used in textile border design

## Colour and Style

Frieze patterns are found across all colour registers — classical stone-coloured monochrome (imitating architectural relief), two-colour geometric, and multicolour decorative. In wallpaper applications, the frieze border is often a coordinated companion to an all-over field paper, designed in matching or complementary palette. Tile frieze borders typically use a contrasting palette to frame the primary tile field.

## History and Context

The architectural frieze has been used as a site for continuous decorative ornament since at least ancient Mesopotamia. Classical Greek architecture formalised the frieze band in temple design — the Elgin Marbles (Parthenon frieze, c. 447–433 BC) are among the most studied examples. Greek pottery from the Geometric period (c. 900–700 BC) uses frieze band compositions for figural and abstract decoration. Indian temple architecture (Khajuraho, Konark) uses elaborate figurative friezes. Persian architectural tile work uses geometric and arabesque frieze bands extensively. In decorative arts and surface design, the frieze format was codified by the Arts and Crafts and Art Nouveau movements, both of which produced extensive ornamental border designs for wallpaper, tile, and textile applications.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Wallpaper borders** — the frieze border paper (a narrow horizontal strip printed for ceiling-level or dado-level placement) is a major commercial wallpaper category, particularly in Victorian and Edwardian revival styles
- **Tile borders** — ceramic and porcelain border tiles use frieze pattern continuously; a key format in bathroom and kitchen tiling
- **Textile trim** — woven, embroidered, or printed border trims on garments and furnishing textiles follow frieze repeat logic
- **Carpet and rug borders** — most rug compositions include a frieze border strip around the central field
- **Stationery** — decorative border designs for letterheads, certificates, and invitations often use frieze pattern structure
