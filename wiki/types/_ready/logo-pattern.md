# Logo Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/logo-pattern/profile/logo-pattern-image.svg)
A surface pattern in which a brand's logo, monogram, or wordmark is used as the primary repeating motif — commercially established as a luxury brand identifier and increasingly used across brand identity, licensing, and commercial design.

## Overview

A logo pattern (also called a brand pattern or monogram repeat) uses a company's logo, monogram, or other brand identifier as the primary repeating element in an all-over surface design. The most commercially established examples — Louis Vuitton's LV monogram, Burberry's chequered check, Gucci's GG pattern, and Adidas's three-stripe mark — demonstrate how a consistently applied repeat pattern can become a brand identity asset as powerful as the logo itself. In surface pattern design, logo patterns represent the intersection of graphic design, brand identity, and textile/product design, and are commercially relevant both as aspirational references for surface designers and as a category for designers working in brand identity and licensing.

## Visual Characteristics

Logo patterns use brand graphic elements — letterforms, monograms, or geometric brand marks — as repeating motifs in an all-over surface. The visual character depends entirely on the brand element used: a monogram repeat (LV, GG) reads as a refined graphic grid; a wordmark repeat uses the full brand name or abbreviated form; a geometric mark repeat (Adidas stripes, Burberry check) uses the brand's visual identity without explicit letterforms. Logo patterns typically rely on flat, graphic rendering rather than pictorial or dimensional illustration — the brand graphic element must remain legible at repeat.

## Pattern Structure

Logo patterns use a variety of repeat arrangements:
- **Regular grid** — motifs placed at even intervals on a square grid; the standard format for most monogram repeats
- **Half-drop** — motifs staggered in alternating rows; creates a denser, more dynamic surface
- **Alternating orientation** — motifs rotated 90° or 180° in alternating positions; used in some monogram designs
- **Diagonal/diamond grid** — motifs placed on a 45° grid; creates a more dynamic surface than orthogonal arrangement

Rhythm types documented in design literature:
- **Flowing rhythm** (after Klimt's Tree of Life): continuous, directional movement through the pattern
- **Random rhythm** (after Mondrian's Broadway Boogie Woogie): non-systematic distribution with visual energy
- **Progressive rhythm** (after Vasarely's Alom): systematic change in scale or form across the repeat
- **Regular rhythm** (after Christo's Thousand Gates): identical elements at consistent intervals
- **Alternating rhythm** (after Delaunay's Endless Rhythm): regular alternation of two elements or orientations

## Common Motifs

Logo pattern motifs are brand-specific, but the category encompasses:
- Monogram letterforms (LV, GG, CC, MK, etc.) — luxury fashion brand shorthand
- Brand marks and symbols — Adidas three stripes, Nike swoosh, Lacoste crocodile in repeat
- Wordmarks in repeat — full or abbreviated brand name repeated across surface
- Abstract brand patterns — Burberry's plaid check pattern (which is a brand identifier rather than a named character motif)
- Pixel/icon motifs — Susan Kare's 1980s Apple Macintosh icons as early examples of interface iconography used in pattern
- Collage elements — Matisse-derived paper cutout forms used in pattern composition (as referenced in logo design literature)

## Colour and Style

Logo pattern colour is brand-dictated. The most commercially significant examples use highly distinctive colour/pattern combinations:
- Louis Vuitton: brown/tan/gold monogram on natural leather ground
- Burberry: the distinctive camel/black/red/white check
- Adidas: black three stripes on white (or brand-colourway combinations)
- Gucci: GG monogram in tan on brown ground

Contemporary brand pattern extends beyond luxury heritage into a wide range of commercial applications, with any brand colour system applicable.

## History and Context

Monogram patterns on textiles have a long history — woven monogram and heraldic repeats in historical European textiles predate modern commercial branding. The modern logo-as-pattern concept was established most durably by Louis Vuitton, whose LV monogram canvas was designed in 1896 by Georges Vuitton to counter counterfeiting — an anti-counterfeiting measure that became the world's most copied luxury pattern. The Burberry plaid check was introduced as a lining pattern in 1924 and has become one of the most recognized brand pattern identifiers in fashion. The development of digital design tools — Adobe Illustrator's pattern maker, swatches panel, and transform effects — has made systematic logo pattern development accessible to any brand identity designer.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Brand merchandise** — logo pattern is used on brand shopping bags, packaging, and branded product ranges
- **Fashion and accessories** — monogram repeat fabric and leather are core production materials for luxury fashion accessories (bags, wallets, scarves)
- **Licensing** — brand pattern motifs are among the highest-value licensed surface design properties; logo pattern licensing is a major revenue stream for luxury brands
- **Stationery and gifting** — brand pattern is commercially active in licensed stationery, gifting, and home products
