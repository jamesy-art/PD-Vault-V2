# Quatrefoil Pattern

## Profile Image

![pd-profile|160](../../assets/types/quatrefoil-pattern/profile/quatrefoil-pattern-image.svg)
A symmetrical decorative shape formed by four partially overlapping circles of equal diameter, creating a four-lobed form; one of the foundational motifs of Gothic architecture, widely used in surface pattern design across textiles, wallpaper, tile, and luxury goods.

## Overview

The quatrefoil (from Old French: "four leaves") is a specific geometric shape: four circles of equal diameter, each overlapping its neighbours, arranged so their centres define a square. The resulting outline is a four-lobed form with pointed intersections between adjacent lobes — resembling a four-leaf clover, a four-petalled flower, or a rounded cross.

As a decorative motif it has been used continuously across at least 1,500 years, appearing in early Christian architecture, Islamic design, Gothic tracery, Renaissance decorative arts, and contemporary interiors. It carries consistent cultural associations with heritage, formality, and luxury, positioning it commercially as a premium interior and fashion motif.

## Visual Characteristics

The quatrefoil is defined by its four equal lobes, the symmetry across both horizontal and vertical axes, and the characteristic pointed cusps where adjacent lobes meet. Variations alter the degree of overlap between the constituent circles:

- **More overlap:** Shorter, rounder lobes; the shape reads as more compact and floral
- **Less overlap:** Longer, more open lobes; the shape reads as more open and architecturally Gothic

In surface pattern repeat, quatrefoils can be:
- **Isolated on plain ground:** Individual quatrefoils in a regular grid on a contrasting ground
- **Interlocking all-over:** Adjacent quatrefoils sharing their arcs, filling the plane without gaps
- **Trellis / lattice:** Quatrefoils connected by thin stem lines in a trellis arrangement

The interlocking all-over variant produces secondary shapes (pointed stars or crosses) in the negative space between quatrefoils — a particularly rich design opportunity.

## Pattern Structure

In all-over repeat, the quatrefoil most commonly uses a square grid base. Each quatrefoil unit occupies a grid cell; adjacent units may be isolated (with visible negative ground between them) or interlocked (sharing their arcs to fill the plane). The interlocked variant is a true tessellation; the isolated variant is a motif repeat.

Variant forms include:
- **Standard quatrefoil:** Four rounded equal lobes; the classic form
- **Barbed quatrefoil:** The intersections between lobes are pinched or notched, creating points that project outward; associated with heraldic imagery and thorned heraldic roses
- **Squarish quatrefoil:** Four lobes inscribed neatly within a square; frequent in Gothic window tracery
- **Double quatrefoil (octofoil):** Eight lobes; the heraldic mark of cadency for a ninth son in English heraldry

## Common Motifs

The quatrefoil is primarily a structural/outline motif; its commercial use is typically as a form that frames or repeats rather than as a figurative image:

- **Interlocking quatrefoil grid:** The all-over woven or printed structure used in textiles and wallpaper
- **Quatrefoil medallion:** Single large quatrefoil as a central or focal decorative element
- **Botanical fill:** Flower or leaf motifs contained within individual quatrefoil cells
- **Barbed quatrefoil:** Heraldic and architectural reference; the Gothic/Renaissance form
- **Stained glass reference:** Coloured geometric fills within a tracery quatrefoil structure

## Colour and Style

Traditional palette: navy, emerald, burgundy, or gold on cream or white ground; black on white for graphic applications. The motif's Gothic heritage positions it naturally in deep, rich colour combinations.

Contemporary applications have expanded the palette to neutrals (grey on cream, white on pale ground for minimalist modern interiors), pastels (soft blue/green/pink for bedroom and bathroom applications), and bold graphic (black and white or high-contrast two-colour).

## History and Context

The quatrefoil predates Gothic architecture by centuries — examples from the Monastery of Stoudios in Constantinople (462 AD) and from Olmec and Mayan monuments in Mesoamerica (some dating to approximately 850 BC) demonstrate that the form developed independently across multiple cultures. In Mesoamerican iconography, the quatrefoil represented the cosmic axis and the passageway between celestial and underworld realms, associated with water and agricultural fertility.

In European design, the quatrefoil achieved its peak prominence during the Gothic and Renaissance periods. It appears as tracery in Gothic architecture (windows, stone screens, metalwork) from the 13th century onwards, with notable early examples at Notre Dame de Paris (barbed quatrefoil, 1260) and in the works of Andrea Pisano and Lorenzo Ghiberti on the Florence Baptistery doors.

The motif has been in continuous commercial use in textiles and wallpaper from the medieval period to the present. In recent years, several luxury brands have attempted to assert trademark rights over the quatrefoil shape; these claims have been contested, as the symbol significantly predates any modern brand's use.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Wallpaper:** One of the most commercially persistent geometric motifs in residential and hospitality wallpaper
- **Home textiles:** Upholstery fabric, cushion covers, bathroom textiles; traditional and contemporary positioning
- **Tile:** Floor and wall tile; the interlocking quatrefoil produces a particularly elegant tile pattern
- **Fashion accessories:** Scarves, bags, jewellery; heritage luxury positioning
- **Ceramics and tableware:** Quatrefoil-shaped serving dishes; pattern applied to ceramic surfaces
- **Licensing:** A reliable commercial pattern in traditional, heritage, and luxury design libraries
