# Hudson's Bay

*[LIQUIDATED 2025]* Canadian department store company and former fur trade conglomerate; founded 1670 by royal charter (King Charles II); oldest corporation in North America (355 years); filed creditor protection March 2025; all stores closed June 2025; intellectual property (including the HBC stripes pattern) sold to Canadian Tire ($30M, May 2025); renamed 1242939 B.C. Unlimited Liability Co. (August 2025).

## Overview

Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) was chartered on May 2, 1670 by King Charles II, granting trading and governance rights over Rupert's Land — a drainage basin encompassing roughly 40% of what is now Canada. For two centuries, HBC was the effective colonial authority and monopoly fur trader across British North America. In 1869, HBC surrendered Rupert's Land to the Dominion of Canada. The first department store opened in Winnipeg in 1881; the chain was rebranded "the Bay" in 1965, and "Hudson's Bay" in 2013. In the 2010s, HBC assembled a multi-banner retail empire: Lord & Taylor (2012), Saks Fifth Avenue (2013, US$2.9B), Galeria Kaufhof and Galeria Inno (2015, US$3.2B), Gilt Groupe (2016). Most were sold or wound down between 2018 and 2024; Saks Fifth Avenue and Saks Off 5th were spun into Saks Global in November 2024. HBC filed for creditor protection under the CCAA on 7 March 2025 — citing trade war pressure, COVID-19 retail disruption, and inability to secure financing. All 74 Hudson's Bay stores, 2 Saks Fifth Avenue stores, and 13 Saks Off 5th outlets were closed by 1 June 2025. HBC was renamed 1242939 B.C. Unlimited Liability Co. in August 2025 to clear the HBC trademark acquired by Canadian Tire. By February 2026, the company employed 8 people (down from 9,364 at creditor protection filing). ## Company Profile

Ownership timeline: Jerry Zucker / private (2006–2008); NRDC Equity Partners (2008–2012); IPO on Toronto Stock Exchange (2012); private again under Richard Baker consortium (March 2020); creditor protection and dissolution (March–August 2025). CEO succession: Jerry Zucker (2006), Anita Zucker (2008), Rob Johnston (2008), Bonnie Brooks (2012), Helena Foulkes (2018), Richard Baker (2020). Subsidiary/banner history: Hudson's Bay (1881–2025), Zellers (1978–2013, brief 2022–2025 revival), Home Outfitters (1999–2019), Lord & Taylor (2012–2019), Saks Fifth Avenue (2013–2024), Saks Off 5th (2013–2024), Gilt Groupe (2016–2018), Galeria Kaufhof (2015–2019), Galeria Inno (2015–2019). As official outfitter of the Canadian Olympic team (2005–2020), HBC produced uniforms, knitwear, and accessories for 10 Olympics, outbidding Roots Canada.

## Market Position

In its peak retail period (2013–2018), HBC was one of North America's largest department store conglomerates by store count and brand portfolio — encompassing Canadian mid-market (Hudson's Bay), American luxury (Saks Fifth Avenue, Lord & Taylor), American off-price (Saks Off 5th), German mid-market (Galeria Kaufhof), and flash sale digital (Gilt Groupe). HBC's collapse in 2025 represented the end of Canada's most historically significant retail institution, accelerated by the 2025 US-Canada trade war and structural retail industry pressures.

## Pattern and Surface Design Relevance

HBC's most significant SPD contribution is the Hudson's Bay Point Blanket:

- **Hudson's Bay Point Blanket (from c. 1700)**: A heavy wool blanket with a distinctive multi-stripe colourway — typically cream ground with broad stripes of indigo, red, green, and yellow. The term "point" (French: *point*) referred to indigo short stripes woven into the blanket edge to indicate size: larger blankets carried more points. By 1700, the point blanket accounted for more than 60% of HBC's trading volume with Indigenous peoples, exchanged for beaver pelts. The blanket became one of the most widely recognised and reproduced branded textile surface designs in North American history — applied to blankets, coats, bags, hats, and fashion collaborations across two centuries. The HBC stripes pattern is now owned by Canadian Tire (IP acquisition, May 2025, $30M), which began marketing HBC Point Blankets from October 2025.
- **Olympic knitwear and graphic design**: As official Canadian Olympic outfitter (2006–2020), HBC produced high-volume pattern objects — the 2010 Vancouver Winter Olympics red mittens (maple leaf motif, $10, 2.6 million pairs sold) and "Canada" hoodies were among the highest-volume pattern objects in the brand's retail history. The 2006/2008 Olympic uniforms used a multicoloured stripe system (green, red, yellow, blue) that was widely criticised as brand advertising rather than national sports identity. A 2010 Cowichan sweater imitation prompted a high-profile cultural appropriation dispute with the Cowichan Tribes.
- **Fur trade and luxury textile commerce**: HBC's fur trade monopoly drove global commerce in luxury animal pelts used in European fashion — beaver, fox, mink, otter. This makes HBC a foundational commercial node in the history of luxury textile supply, predating and enabling the European fashion industry's access to North American furs.

## Design Direction

No ongoing creative design function — company liquidated 2025. Historically, HBC's design function centred on Olympic uniforms, own-brand fashion, and the Hudson's Bay Point Blanket colourway. The point blanket stripe system is now the company's most commercially durable design legacy, having transferred intact to Canadian Tire's IP portfolio.

## Retail and Licensing Context

HBC's IP — including the Hudson's Bay name, trademarks, logos, coat of arms, and stripes pattern — purchased by Canadian Tire for $30 million (May 2025). The 1670 royal charter auctioned upon dissolution; Weston family and Thomson family jointly paid $18 million and donated it to Archives of Manitoba, Manitoba Museum, Canadian Museum of History, and Royal Ontario Museum (December 2025).
