# Warnaco Group

Defunct American apparel licensing and manufacturing corporation; formerly operated and licensed Calvin Klein, Speedo, Warner's, and Olga brands at global scale. Acquired by [[PVH Corp.]] in February 2013.

## Overview

The Warnaco Group, Inc. was an American textile and apparel corporation that designed, sourced, marketed, licensed, and distributed underwear, sportswear, and swimwear worldwide. The company traces its origins to Warner Brothers Corset Manufacturers, founded in 1874 in Bridgeport, Connecticut by brothers Lucien and Ira De Ver Warner. Renamed Warnaco in 1968, the group grew through brand acquisitions and licensing deals into a multinational conglomerate.

Under CEO Linda Wachner from 1986, the company expanded aggressively — acquiring Calvin Klein jeans and underwear licences, Speedo, and other brands — before filing for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2001 following overextension and financial irregularities. Warnaco emerged from bankruptcy in 2003, stabilised around the Calvin Klein franchise, and was acquired by [[PVH Corp.]] for $2.8 billion in October 2012 (completed February 2013). The company no longer operates independently.

## Company Profile

- **Founded:** 1874 (as Warner Brothers Corset Manufacturers); renamed Warnaco 1968
- **Dissolved:** Acquired by [[PVH Corp.]] February 2013
- **Primary brands operated:** Calvin Klein (jeans, underwear, swimwear), Speedo, Warner's, Olga, Chaps
- **Peak revenue:** $1.95 billion (1998)
- **2009 net revenues:** $2 billion; Calvin Klein accounted for approximately 75%

## Market Position

At its peak, Warnaco was one of the largest apparel licensing vehicles in the United States, operating across three divisions — Intimates, Swimwear, and Sportswear. The company operated over 1,000 Calvin Klein retail stores worldwide and franchised a further 624. Calvin Klein jeans were distributed across North America, Europe, and Asia under licence agreements.

## Products and Categories

- Underwear and intimates — Calvin Klein Underwear, Warner's, Olga
- Jeans and jeanswear — Calvin Klein (North/South/Central America, Europe, Asia)
- Swimwear — Calvin Klein (worldwide), Speedo (US, Canada, Mexico, Caribbean)
- Sleepwear and loungewear — Calvin Klein
- Menswear — Chaps by Ralph Lauren (relinquished to Ralph Lauren Corporation on PVH acquisition)

## Pattern and Surface Design Relevance

Warnaco functioned as a large-scale commercial vehicle for its licensed brands' visual identities, operating at the intersection of print, branding, and mass-market distribution. Calvin Klein jeans and swimwear — managed under Warnaco's licences — are categories where fabric identity, surface graphics, and colourway design are central to product differentiation and retail positioning. Speedo swimwear required print and colourway design applied to performance stretch fabrics at commercial volume.

A foundational structural contribution: a 1959 partnership between Warnaco and DuPont led to the development of Lycra — a stretch material that transformed how prints and fitted surface designs could be applied across swimwear and intimate apparel. Warner's and Olga lingerie incorporated surface design through lace construction, printed fabric, and garment-level decoration across their market histories.

## Design Direction

Licensing-driven. Creative direction resided with brand owners — Calvin Klein, Speedo's parent company, Ralph Lauren for Chaps — while Warnaco's role was manufacturing compliance and global distribution. A 2000 lawsuit by Calvin Klein, alleging Warnaco sold CK jeans through unapproved discount channels, illustrates how licensing structures define and constrain the commercial surface presentation of fashion brands.

## Retail and Licensing Context

Owned trademarks included Warner's, Olga, and Calvin Klein underwear and loungewear (beneficially owned). Perpetual licences included Speedo for the US, Canada, Mexico, and Caribbean. Term licences covered Calvin Klein jeans and retail stores across the Americas, Europe, and Asia — all passing to [[PVH Corp.]] upon acquisition. The Warner's and Olga brands were subsequently sold by PVH to Basic Resources, Inc. in November 2023.

**The Warnaco Group, Inc.** was an American [textile/clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_industry "Textile industry") [corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation "Corporation") which designed, sourced, marketed, [licensed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_licensing "Brand licensing"), and distributed a wide range of [underwear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwear "Underwear"), [sportswear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sportswear_\(fashion\) "Sportswear (fashion)"), and [swimwear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimwear "Swimwear") worldwide. Its products were sold under several [brand names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand "Brand") including [Calvin Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_Klein_\(fashion_house\) "Calvin Klein (fashion house)"), [Speedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speedo "Speedo"), [Chaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaps_\(brand\) "Chaps (brand)"), Warner's, and Olga.

On 31 October 2012, the company announced that it would be acquired by [PVH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PVH_Corp. "PVH Corp.") for $2.8 billion in cash and stock.[^6]

The deal gave PVH more control of the Calvin Klein clothing brand as it will unite Calvin Klein formal, underwear, jeans and sportswear lines. It was acquired by PVH in Feb 2013.[^7]

In November 2023, PVH sold the Warners and Olga brands to Basic Resources, Inc.[^8]

## History

### Dr. Warner's Health Corsets

In the late 19th century, Dr. Lucien Warner, a prominent physician gave up his [Cortlandville, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortlandville,_New_York "Cortlandville, New York"), practice to begin a new career on the medical lecturing circuit, specializing in women's health issues. Dr. Warner lectured about the [harmful effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corset_controversy "Corset controversy") of the rigid [steel-boned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_\(corsetry\) "Bone (corsetry)") [corsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corset "Corset") of the time. After seeing how little influence his lectures had on women's attitudes towards fashion, he returned to his New York home and began a more aggressive approach to fighting the ills caused by the corset.[^9]

In 1873, he designed a corset that provided both the shape desired by women and the flexibility required to allow some movement and reduce injuries caused by previous designs. The next year, Lucien Warner and his brother Dr. Ira De Ver Warner gave up their medical practices and founded Warner Brothers Corset Manufacturers.[^10]

Dr. Warner's Coraline Health Corsets, as they were marketed,[^11] were made up of two pieces of cloth which were laced or clasped together. These revolutionary undergarments also featured shoulder straps and more flexible boning and lateral bust supports [^12] made of Coraline, a product of the fibers of the Mexican [Ixtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixtle "Ixtle") plant.[^13] [^14] By 1876, this new, more flexible design had grown in popularity so much so that the company moved its manufacturing operations to [Bridgeport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeport,_Connecticut "Bridgeport, Connecticut"), [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut "Connecticut"), where approximately 1,200 people were employed to produce approximately 6,000 corsets daily.[^15] In 1883, *[Harper's Bazaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper%27s_Bazaar "Harper's Bazaar")* advertised the four most popular corsets in America as Dr. Warner's models [^16] The brothers claimed patents and trademarks on "health corset" and they had international manufacturing.[^17] The success of the Warners' designs had made the brothers millionaires and in 1894 they retired and turned control of the company over to De Ver's son, D.H. and the Warner Brothers partnership was changed to a corporation.

### The Warner Brothers Corset Co.

The turn of the century saw even greater success for the company in the hands of the founders' sons. New products included the rust-proof corset and combination corset and hose-supporter. By 1913 sales reached $7 million and profits averaged $700,000 annually [^18] Two years later, The Warner Brothers Corset Co. paid $1,500 for [Mary Phelps Jacob's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Phelps_Jacob "Mary Phelps Jacob") patent for the [brassiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra "Bra") - a move which helped boost revenues to $12.6 million by 1920.[^18]

The [Jazz Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_Age "Jazz Age") and [Flapper movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flapper "Flapper") of the 1920s saw the desire for less restrictive fashions. Women had a more care-free attitude toward life and ditched the corset and pantaloons in favor of breast-binding [bandeaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandeau "Bandeau") and step-in [panties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panties "Panties").[^19]

This was a difficult time for the company. Sales through the decade declined and efforts made by the company to adapt to these changing times were met with little success.

### Depression Era

The [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression") of the 1930s was difficult on the clothing industry and Warner was no exception to this financial suffering. Even as the boyish figure of the previous decade's Flappers fell out of style and curves made a return to fashion, Warner struggled. By 1932, the company had lost more than $1 million.[^18] The company's troubles were only made worse by the personal deterioration of CEO, D. H. Warner, who was known as a depraved womanizer.[^18] After his wife died in 1931, D.H. continued to finance his debauchery with company profits and drink to excess before dying in 1934 at the age of 66. Control of the company was handed to his son-in-law, John Field.[^18]

### ABC and IPO

With the corset all but extinct by the mid-1930s, the company's new leadership focused on developing new products. In 1937, the company that revolutionized corsetry revolutionized the brassiere by assigning letters to various cup sizes.[^20] The ABC Alphabet Bra set the standard for [bra sizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brassiere_measurement "Brassiere measurement") that is still used today. By the early 1940s, the company was profitable again, bringing in $1 million by 1947.[^18] Sales of bras, girdles, and the [cross-promotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-promotion "Cross-promotion") of the Merry Widow line of [corselets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corselette "Corselette") with the 1952 [Lana Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lana_Turner "Lana Turner") movie of [the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merry_Widow_\(1952_film\) "The Merry Widow (1952 film)"), led to record profits.[^18]

A partnership between [DuPont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuPont "DuPont") and Warners lead to the 1959 invention of [Lycra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycra "Lycra"), which allowed for new designs in shapewear and more snug-fitting bras.[^21] The late 1950s also saw Warner Brothers diversify its product lineup to include menswear and accessories, as well as sportswear for both men and women. Distribution was expanded by sales in large [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_store "Chain store") [department stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store "Department store") such as [JC Penney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JCPenney "JCPenney") and [Sears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears "Sears"). The company also expanded production, opening manufacturing facilities in [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America "South America") and [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"). In 1960, Warner Brothers Company purchased major American shirt manufacturer [C.F. Hathaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.F._Hathaway_Company "C.F. Hathaway Company") strengthening the company's foothold in the sportswear market. Warner Brothers [went public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_public_offering "Initial public offering") in 1961 and was soon generating revenues in excess of $100 million.[^18] In 1966, it acquired another large clothing maker, the [White Stag Manufacturing Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Stag_\(clothing\) "White Stag (clothing)").[^22]

### Warnaco

The Warner Brothers Company changed its name to Warnaco, Inc. in 1968, and continued to grow its business exponentially through various mergers and acquisitions throughout the 1970s. By the middle of the decade, Warnaco had become a multi-national clothing conglomerate with almost 20 divisions. Despite the company's diverse portfolio, however, Warnaco was struggling to turn a profit. Recognizing the potential failure, Field handed management of the company over to James Walker and Philip Lamoureux. Walker was named CEO in 1977.[^23] [^24]

Lamoureux and Walker turned the company around quickly and in 1982, Lamoureux left the company. A year later, Walker died unexpectedly.[^18] That year brought in $28.3 million.[^18] However, some of the cost-cutting measures implemented by Lamoureux and Walker — including cutbacks in research and in advertising — hurt the company more than helping it. In 1986, after being away from the company for nine years,[^25] former lingerie division president [Linda J. Wachner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_J._Wachner "Linda J. Wachner") engineered a $550 million [hostile takeover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostile_takeover "Hostile takeover"). Wachner had previously risen through the ranks at [Max Factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Factor "Max Factor"), making the declining [cosmetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmetics "Cosmetics") company profitable again within just two years.[^25] She wasted no time at Warnaco and right away went to work streamlining the company's fifteen divisions into just two categories: menswear and underwear.

### The Wachner years

In 1990, Wachner formed a new corporation, [Authentic Fitness Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Authentic_Fitness_Corp.&action=edit&redlink=1 "Authentic Fitness Corp. (page does not exist)"), for the purpose of separating Warnaco's activewear lines including [Speedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speedo "Speedo") and [White Stag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Stag_\(clothing\) "White Stag (clothing)") ski clothing. Wachner's intention was to transform Speedo from swimwear label to retail concept.[^26] Authentic fitness went public in June 1992 and opened its first Speedo Authentic retail store five months later [^26] In 1993, Authentic Fitness had a licensing deal with [Oscar de la Renta, Ltd.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_de_la_Renta "Oscar de la Renta") and had acquired swimwear labels Cole, Catalina, and Anne Cole — each from [bankruptcies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankruptcy "Bankruptcy"). That same year, Wachner secured a sponsorship deal for the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics "1996 Summer Olympics") in [Atlanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlanta,_Georgia "Atlanta, Georgia"), [GA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_\(U.S._state\) "Georgia (U.S. state)").[^26]

By 1991, Warnaco's lingerie division had license agreements with Valentino, Ungaro, Scaasi, [Bob Mackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Mackie "Bob Mackie"), [Victoria's Secret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria%27s_Secret "Victoria's Secret") and [Fruit of the Loom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_of_the_Loom "Fruit of the Loom"). The menswear division produced shirts, sweaters, neckties and other accessories under names including [Christian Dior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Dior "Christian Dior"), Hathaway, Chaps by [Ralph Lauren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Lauren "Ralph Lauren"), and [Jack Nicklaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Nicklaus "Jack Nicklaus"). At the end of 1991, the company produced $195.4 million in gross profits and Linda Wachner was named Business Woman of the Year by [*Fortune*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_\(magazine\) "Fortune (magazine)").[^27]

The remainder of the decade saw Wachner take her company on a buying spree, acquiring designer labels Calvin Klein Underwear, Body Slimmers (purchased from Nancy Ganz, wife of [Mitchell S. Steir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_S._Steir "Mitchell S. Steir")), ABS by Allan Schwartz, as well as [private label](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_label "Private label") sleepwear manufacturers GJM Group, French lingerie company Lejaby-Euralis.[^28] Warnaco also acquired the license for Calvin Klein Jeans and Calvin Klein retail stores through its takeover of Designer Holdings, Inc.[^29] The license for Calvin Klein children's clothing was purchased from Commerce Clothing [^30] Warnaco closed out the 1990s by selling off its underperforming Hathaway label and reacquiring Authentic Fitness.

The company's success peaked in 1998 with $1.95 billion in revenue.[^18] Soon after, however, sales dropped rapidly and — saddled with debt from all the recent acquisitions and mergers — in 2000, the company lost $200 million.[^31] In 2001, Warnaco filed for [Chapter 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_11,_Title_11,_United_States_Code "Chapter 11, Title 11, United States Code") protection [^32] [^33] [^34] [^35] and Wachner was fired.[^36] [^37] [^38]

### A new beginning

On 4 February 2003, Warnaco emerged from bankruptcy. As part of its restructuring, the company sold its [White Stag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Stag_\(clothing\) "White Stag (clothing)") trademark to [Wal-Mart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmart "Walmart") and later decided to exit the designer swimwear market and focus on strengthening its Speedo products.

The company sold off [Ocean Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_Pacific "Ocean Pacific") to [Iconix Brand Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconix_Brand_Group "Iconix Brand Group") after just three years of ownership.[^39] Also sold, were [Catalina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalina_Swimwear "Catalina Swimwear"), [Anne Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Cole "Anne Cole") and [Cole of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cole_of_California "Cole of California") brands. This netted the company approximately $25 million.[^40]

In 2008, the company also ceased operations under the [Michael Kors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Kors "Michael Kors") and [Nautica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautica "Nautica") labels, citing a collective $1.7 million in losses from the two brands.[^40] In further efforts to boost its swimwear line, Speedo renewed its contract with 8-time [Olympic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games "Olympic Games") [gold medalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_medal "Gold medal") [Michael Phelps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Phelps "Michael Phelps"), extending his endorsement through the next [Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics "2012 Summer Olympics").[^41]

Warnaco provides private label swimsuits for Victoria's Secret.[^40] [^42]

In order to strengthen its "core intimates" group, (Warner's, Olga, Calvin Klein Underwear), the company shed private label GJM [^43] and high-end lingerie brand Lejaby.[^44]

Since emerging from bankruptcy, Warnaco Group's annual income Reports have shown steady growth.[^45]

Calvin Klein continues to be a strong performer for the company in both the jeans and intimates sectors. As of 2 January 2010, the company operated over 1,000 Calvin Klein retail stores worldwide as well as three online stores.[^40] [^46] It also licenses or franchises an additional 624 stores and the Calvin Klein brand accounted for 75% of the Warnaco Group's $2 billion net sales in 2009.[^46]

At the end of 2010's second quarter (ending 3 July), Warnaco reported that all three divisions — Intimates, Swimwear and Sportswear — contributed to its 14% growth in net revenues to $519.3 million [^47] and industry analysts expect continued growth.[^46] [^47] [^48] [^49] [^50] In August 2010 *[The Motley Fool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Motley_Fool "The Motley Fool")* named Warnaco one of its Top 10 Values in Consumer Durables, citing the stock's low price-to-earnings multiples as well as its low risk and its potential for growth.[^51]

## Controversies and criticisms

### Bridgeport Strike of 1915

During the summer of 1915, approximately 1,300 women and girls employed by The Warner Brothers Corset Company factory in [Bridgeport, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeport,_Connecticut "Bridgeport, Connecticut"), [walked off the job](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkout "Walkout").[^52] The [strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strike_action "Strike action"), which was one of 179 strikes recorded that year,[^53] was in favor of [eight-hour work days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day "Eight-hour day") [^52] and a 20% increase in wages.[^54] On 18 August that year, the nearly 4,000 striking workers — who were organized as part of the [International Textile Workers of America union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_Workers_Union_of_America "Textile Workers Union of America") — accepted the management's offer of a 12.5% raise and eight-hour days.[^55]

One worker was reported injured during this strike. Connecticut newspaper *[The Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Day_\(New_London\) "The Day (New London)")* reported "a Miss Jones...who is believed to have objected to the \[negotiation\] proceedings, is said to have been roughly handled by the other strikers, and to have had her clothing almost torn from her." No one was arrested for this attack.[^52]

### Made in the U.S.A.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Saipan_Garment_Factory_1.JPG/250px-Saipan_Garment_Factory_1.JPG)

Loading ramp of garment factory on Saipan

Early in 1999, Warnaco was one of 18 companies initially named in three [class-action lawsuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_action "Class action") filed under US Federal [RICO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racketeer_Influenced_and_Corrupt_Organizations_Act "Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act") statutes. The lawsuits were filed by several labor and human rights groups on behalf of more than 50,000 workers from [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines "Philippines"), [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh") and [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand").[^56] [^57] Ultimately 26 U.S. companies and 23 Saipan garment factories would be named as defendants.[^57]

The suits claimed that the garment factories — located in [Saipan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saipan "Saipan"), [Northern Mariana Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Mariana_Islands "Northern Mariana Islands"), a [United States Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_\(U.S._insular_area\) "Commonwealth (U.S. insular area)") — regularly employed immigrant laborers who were duped into paying a "recruiting fee" of up to $7,000 [^57] so they can work in The United States. Upon arrival in Saipan, the workers are forced to surrender their passports and work off the money they owe, in effect making them [indentured servants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indentured_servitude "Indentured servitude"). These factories produce clothing for the companies named in the suit. The workers further claimed that they were forced to sign "shadow contracts" waiving basic human rights, including the freedom to date or marry. And they emphasize the poor working and living conditions for workers. The suits allege they work and live in crowded, unsanitary factories and shanty-like housing compounds that are in flagrant violation of federal law.[^56]

Almost immediately, Warnaco denied any wrongdoing, stating that they hire subcontractors that strictly follow U.S. law [^58] and in the spring of 1999, was among the first companies to settle.[^59] By 2004 all remaining companies – with the exception of [Levi Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_Strauss_%26_Co. "Levi Strauss & Co.") whose case was ultimately dismissed – had settled without admitting to any wrongdoing. The Saipan garment workers had won a collective $20 million as well as better oversight and improved working conditions.[^60]

### SEC investigation

On 11 May 2004, the [Securities and Exchange Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Securities_and_Exchange_Commission "U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission") announced a settlement in its three-year-long investigation of Warnaco and its auditing firm, [PricewaterhouseCoopers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PwC "PwC"). The investigation stemmed from an overstatement of $145 million worth of inventory on Warnaco's 1998 [Form 10-K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_10-K "Form 10-K").[^61] The SEC alleged that Warnaco knew about this overstatement when they released a press statement lauding the company's "record results" for its fourth quarter and fiscal year ending 1998. This overstatement would force Warnaco to restate its earnings for the three prior years. The commission complained Warnaco "falsely portrayed the inventory write-down as a part of the company's write-off of deferred start-up costs under a new accounting pronouncement.[^62] "

The complaints filed by the SEC claim PwC's role in this alleged cover-up was that they caught the error during an audit but they "failed to object" to Warnaco's "mischaracterization" of this error and that PwC incorporated this mischaracterization into their own audit report.[^63]

In the suit, the SEC specifically names William Finklestein, who was vice president and CFO at the time of the allegation, claiming "As CFO, Finkelstein directed the offsetting of cash against debt. He also reviewed and signed the quarterly report.[^40] " Linda Wachner was also named in the suit as she was president and CEO at the time. In its press release announcing the settlement of the suit, the commission stated "at the time she approved and signed the 1998 annual report, Wachner knew or should have known that the restatement of the company's financial results was caused by material flaws in the cost accounting and internal control systems at one of the company's largest divisions and was not related to the write-up of deferred start-up or start-up related costs." The commission also found that "Wachner knew or should have known that the division's inventory costing control system was inadequate to ensure the accuracy of Warnaco's books and records and failed to ensure that proper internal controls were in place.[^62] " Stanley P. Silverstein, who was Warnaco's general counsel at the time was also named, as he, too, signed off on the report.

PricewaterhouseCoopers settled with the SEC and agreed to pay a $2.4 million penalty. Wachner was found to have caused the oversight and was ordered to pay $1.3 million in [disgorgement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disgorgement "Disgorgement"). Finkelstein was ordered to pay $189,464 in disgorgement as well as a civil penalty of $75,000. He was barred for four years from serving as an officer or director of a public company. Silverstein was [censured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censure "Censure") and ordered to pay $165,772.[^40]

### Calvin Klein, et al. vs. The Warnaco Group, et al.

On 30 May 2000, Calvin Klein, Inc. filed suit against Warnaco Group, Inc. and its CEO, Linda Wachner. The suit alleged that Warnaco had [diluted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trademark_dilution "Trademark dilution") the Calvin Klein brand name by producing merchandise that was not authorized or approved by Calvin Klein and that Warnaco was distributing Calvin Klein jeanswear through unapproved discount outlets, such as [warehouse clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warehouse_club "Warehouse club"), like [Costco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costco "Costco") and [BJ's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BJ%27s_Wholesale_Club "BJ's Wholesale Club"). [Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_Klein "Calvin Klein") himself appeared on [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN "CNN") 's *[Larry King Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_King_Live "Larry King Live")* shortly after the suit was filed and stated these practices have been taking place since Warnaco acquired the license three years prior. Calvin Klein had sought to regain control of its jeanswear license. Warnaco's initial response to the suit, which [King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_King "Larry King") read on the air, called the complaint "without merit." The statement further accused Calvin Klein of "throwing stones at Warnaco," in a "desperate attempt... to cover up and distract focus from the highly deteriorated business state of CKI".[^64] In response to Klein's television appearance, Warnaco filed a countersuit accusing Klein of trademark libel for not only maligning Warnaco but also Calvin Klein's own products.[^65] The suit was settled in 2001 and sealed with a "fashionista air kiss" [^66] on the steps in front of a New York courthouse.

While the agreement remains confidential, some of the terms have been made public. Warnaco was able to retain its Calvin Klein licenses, but Calvin Klein was able to regain some of the creative control he had ceded in the original license. The agreement opens:

> IT IS HEREBY AGREED by and between Calvin Klein and Warnaco as follows:
> 
> 1\. Calvin Klein and Warnaco agree to work together for their mutual benefit under their existing license and other agreements, except to the extent that those agreements are modified by the terms of this Settlement Agreement.
> 
> 2\. a. Beginning in calendar year 2002, Warnaco will limit its annual gross sales of Calvin Klein jeanswear to mass merchandisers (defined as, for example, KMart, Wal-Mart and Target) and/or warehouse clubs so that the percentage of such sales does not exceed \*\*\*% of Warnaco's total gross sales of Calvin Klein jeanswear in 2002 (and the percentage of sales other than excess and close-outs to such channels does not exceed \*\*\*% of total gross sales) and \*\*\*% in 2003 and thereafter (and the percentage of sales other than excess and close-outs to such channels does not exceed \*\*\*% of total gross sales). \*\*\*.This provision is without prejudice to the positions of the parties as to the meaning of the license terms.[^67]

### Red ink and golden parachute

When Linda Wachner forcibly took over the reins of Warnaco, she reduced the company's debt by 35% within four years.[^68] After the mid-1990s acquisition spree, Wachner led the clothing powerhouse once again to the brink of collapse. It was under her management that in 1995 Warnaco failed to make the [*Fortune* 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_500 "Fortune 500") for the first time in nearly 30 years.

*[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\) "Time (magazine)")* [magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\) "Time (magazine)") referred to Warnaco's reacquisition of Authentic Fitness as "financial gymnastics that helped prop up Wachner's bank account but ultimately loaded Warnaco's balance sheet with an extra $600 million in debt." [^69] " Between 1998 and 2000, Warnaco's stock had lost about 75% of its value, yet Wachner continued to draw a base salary of $2.7 million with an additional $12.5 million in bonuses and private stock.[^70]

Upon her ouster as CEO, Wachner was denied her contractual $25 million [golden parachute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_parachute "Golden parachute"). She sued the company and in 2002 she accepted $3.5 million in new Warnaco stock and $200,000 in cash, which she said she would donate to cancer research.[^71] Wachner stayed on the board of directors until her term expired in 2003.
