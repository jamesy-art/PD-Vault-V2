# Rain Pattern

A surface pattern category using the visual language of rain — droplets, streaks, drips, and downpour lines — as its primary motif; covers both abstract and illustrative approaches, and overlaps with the military camouflage tradition of broken vertical-line "raindrop" patterns.

## Overview

Rain patterns operate across two distinct design registers. The first is an illustrative or abstract surface pattern tradition: raindrop shapes, falling lines, drip motifs, and weather-associated imagery (clouds, umbrellas, puddle reflections) applied to textiles, stationery, and homeware. The second is the military camouflage tradition, in which broken vertical irregular lines on a muted ground produce patterns colloquially known as "raindrop" camouflage — a category with its own textile and design history.

In consumer surface pattern design, rain and drip motifs have consistent commercial applications in children's products (particularly for rainy weather themes), fashion (particularly April/spring collections), and occasion-based stationery (umbrellas and rain for specific greeting card markets).

## Visual Characteristics

The rain motif in surface pattern draws on several visual forms:

- **Droplet shape:** The classic teardrop raindrop silhouette; a rounded base with a pointed top; stylised or naturalistic; used as an isolated repeat unit
- **Falling streak:** Short diagonal or vertical lines suggesting rainfall in motion; creates directionality
- **Drip:** The elongated hanging water drop with a rounded bottom; associated with condensation, heavy rain, or illustrated liquid
- **Ripple / splash:** Concentric circles suggesting a raindrop impact; used in illustrations and as an abstract geometric motif
- **Puddle reflection:** Landscape-scale rain illustration; less common as a seamless repeat element

Camouflage applications use a different visual vocabulary: irregular, broken, roughly vertical dashes or short strokes in muted earth tones (olive, grey-green, brown) that disrupt human silhouette recognition.

## Pattern Structure

Rain patterns use several repeat structures depending on their visual intent:

- **All-over tossed:** Raindrop shapes scattered at varied angles and scales across the ground; the most common approach for non-directional repeat
- **Directional vertical/diagonal stripe:** Falling streak lines in a repeated near-parallel structure; produces a directional repeat with visible top/bottom orientation
- **Regular grid:** Ordered droplet motifs in a diamond or square grid; graphic, geometric application
- **Mixed-element conversational:** Raindrops combined with umbrellas, clouds, boots, puddles; narrative illustration in a tossed layout

## Common Motifs

- **Raindrop shape:** The primary isolated motif; stylised teardrop
- **Falling streak lines:** Short diagonal marks suggesting rainfall; used as texture or all-over surface fill
- **Cloud:** Contextual companion motif in weather-themed compositions
- **Umbrella:** Narrative companion motif; associated with spring and rainy-day themes
- **Water splash / ripple:** Abstract impact motif; geometric concentric arc form
- **Wellington boot / raincoat:** Conversational companions in children's rain-theme prints
- **Broken vertical dash (camouflage):** The military tradition raindrop motif; irregular short strokes in muted tones

## Colour and Style

Consumer rain patterns use a range of approaches:

- **Classic blue palette:** Sky blue, navy, cerulean, teal — the natural colour association with water and rain; common in fashion and stationery
- **Grey-scale:** Atmospheric, minimalist; silver-grey and white on a pale ground for adult fashion or homeware
- **Bright conversational:** Primary colours, pastel grounds; children's products and novelty stationery
- **Monochrome graphic:** Black line drops on white; graphic stationery and branding

Camouflage rain patterns use: grey-green, olive, khaki, brown, and off-white; broken, muted, desaturated.

## History and Context

The military "raindrop" pattern tradition traces directly to East German military camouflage: the *Kampfanzug 64* (combat suit introduced in 1965), made from "Strichtarn" fabric — German for "streak/stripe camouflage." The pattern used broken, slightly curved vertical stripes in reddish-brown and dark brown on a grey-green ground. This camouflage was used by the East German National People's Army (NVA) from 1965 until German reunification in 1990.

Strichtarn was descended from Wehrmacht-era *Splittertarnmuster* ("splinter camouflage") patterns used in World War II, and was developed in parallel with comparable patterns in allied Warsaw Pact countries: Czechoslovak *Jehličí* ("needles" pattern), and Polish *Wz.58 "Deszczyk"* (literally "little rain"). These patterns collectively constitute the Central European "raindrop camouflage" tradition.

In consumer surface pattern design, rain and water motifs have a much longer and broader history, spanning traditional Japanese textile art (water wave and ripple motifs), Dutch Delftware blue-and-white water patterns, Art Nouveau flowing water imagery, and contemporary digital illustration.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Children's apparel and accessories:** Rain-themed clothing (wellingtons, raincoats, umbrellas); seasonal spring/autumn product lines
- **Stationery:** Rain and weather-theme greeting cards, notebooks, wrapping paper; occasion-specific market
- **Home textiles:** Shower curtains (contextually appropriate water/rain motif); towels; bath accessories
- **Fashion fabric:** Blue-palette drip and streak motifs on dress fabric, lining fabric; spring collections
- **Outdoor and technical textile:** Camouflage-derived raindrop patterns in workwear, streetwear, and military-aesthetic fashion
- **Wallpaper:** Abstract streak and droplet patterns for feature walls; contemporary interior use
