# Moiré Pattern

An optical interference pattern produced when two similar ruled or repeating patterns are overlaid at a slight angle or with a slight scale difference — used deliberately in textile production, security printing, and scientific measurement, and an important artefact in digital printing and photography.

## Overview

A moiré pattern is a large-scale visual interference effect that appears when two repeating visual patterns (such as grids, lines, or dots) are overlaid with a slight rotation, offset, or scale difference between them. The visual result is a new, emergent large-scale pattern — typically a series of waves, arcs, or flowing curves — that is not present in either original pattern individually. The moiré effect is both an unintended printing artefact (a problem in halftone printing and digital photography) and a deliberately exploited optical tool in textile production, security printing, and scientific instrumentation. The term derives from the French word for a specific watered-silk textile, which produces a natural moiré effect through its weave structure.

## Visual Characteristics

A moiré pattern produces large-scale wave-like or fringe-like patterns that flow across the surface as a secondary visual layer over the original repeating elements. The emergent pattern can take the form of: concentric rings (when two circular patterns are slightly offset), parallel bands (when two line patterns are slightly rotated), or complex flowing curves (in more complex overlays). The scale of the emergent moiré pattern is inversely related to the degree of mismatch between the two layers — a very slight rotation produces large-scale, slow-moving moiré patterns; a larger angular difference produces finer, faster-moving interference.

## Pattern Structure

Two distinct optical categories of moiré are documented:
- **Line moiré** — produced by overlaying two sets of parallel lines (straight or curved) with a slight angular difference; produces characteristic banding effect; can create optical illusions of movement or acceleration in the pattern
- **Shape moiré** — produced by overlaying shapes with slight offset or scale difference; creates a magnification or distortion effect in specific areas; used in precision measurement applications

## Common Motifs

Moiré is a structural optical phenomenon rather than a motif-based category. Its appearance depends on the underlying patterns being overlaid. In textile applications, the moiré effect on woven fabric (particularly ribbed silk or taffeta) produces a characteristic rippling water-like surface pattern — hence the name from "watered silk." In surface design referencing the moiré effect, designers typically simulate the wave or fringe pattern associated with the optical phenomenon.

## Colour and Style

Moiré textile (woven) is characteristically a single-colour or two-colour sheen effect — the pattern appears through the differential light reflection of the woven surface rather than through chromatic difference. Silk moiré fabric in natural silk colours (cream, ivory, black) is the traditional luxury form. In printed moiré simulation, any colour ground is possible — the moiré wave pattern is typically printed as a lighter or darker tonal variation over a solid ground.

## History and Context

The term "moiré" derives from the French word for a specific watered-silk textile. The French noun for this textile dates to the 17th century and is itself a loanword from English "mohair," reflecting the trade history of luxury textiles between England and France. The watered appearance of moiré silk is produced by pressing two layers of ribbed silk fabric together while damp — the ribbed weave structures create a registration mismatch producing the characteristic water-pattern on the surface. This physical textile technique for producing decorative moiré effects dates to before the 17th century. In modern applications, moiré is used deliberately in security printing — banknotes, passports, and official documents use moiré patterns as anti-counterfeiting measures, since the patterns are disrupted when photocopied or digitally reproduced. Maritime navigation has used projected moiré light patterns to guide ships into harbour. In digital photography and TV production, moiré is an unintended artefact produced when finely-patterned fabric (stripes, fine checks) is captured by a digital sensor — the digital pixel grid interferes with the fabric's pattern grid to produce unwanted moiré banding in the image.

## Surface Pattern Uses

- **Luxury textiles** — moiré silk and taffeta are established luxury fabric categories; watered silk moiré is commercially available for eveningwear and formal interior applications
- **Security printing** — moiré patterns are a standard security feature on banknotes, passports, and official documents; a highly specialised commercial application
- **Surface design reference** — the moiré wave aesthetic is referenced in printed surface design for wallpaper and textiles to produce optical surface depth effects
- **Contemporary digital pattern** — moiré-inspired optical wave patterns are commercially used in contemporary graphic and surface design for their optical depth and movement quality
